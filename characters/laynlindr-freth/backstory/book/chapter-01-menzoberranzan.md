## Chapter 1 — Menzoberranzan

There was no sky above Menzoberranzan, and there had never been one. There was only stone, rising so far above the city that even the sharpest eyes could not find where the cavern ended and darkness began. Menzoberranzan occupied a vast, arrowhead-shaped cavern, its roof nearly a thousand feet above the floor, while great columns and jutting shelves of calcite divided the space into layers. Bridges and walkways crossed the emptiness between them, joining towers carved directly from the living rock.

Some houses clung to the cavern walls, while others rose from the floor in defensive spires. Paths crossed the roof overhead, where powerful magic held homes and galleries against the stone, and from below, the highest districts seemed to hang above the city like a second, inverted world.

At the cavern's center stood Narbondel, a huge natural pillar of stone that served as the city's clock. Each morning, the Archmage warmed the stone to life, and a band of heat climbed slowly up the pillar as the day passed, read from almost every street, bridge, and balcony by eyes that saw warmth as the surface folk saw color. When the heat reached its end and faded into cold, Menzoberranzan entered its long night. No bell announced the hour, and no sun crossed a horizon; the city simply watched the stone.

Blue, green, and violet faerie fire burned across the city, outlining towers, gates, and house compounds in cold radiance, while pale fungi clung to the deeper walls and added their own weak glow. In some districts, the streets were bright enough for traders to display their goods; in others, the light thinned until it became little more than a suggestion against the black.

None of it defeated the darkness. The light only gave the darkness edges.

A balcony could disappear beyond a veil of shadow, a bridge could seem to end where the stone fell away into blackness, and a doorway could stand open for years while no passing eye noticed what lay beyond it. To someone from the surface, Menzoberranzan might have seemed to exist in perpetual night, but to the drow, it was simply home.

And it was a city in every sense of the word.

There were streets crowded with movement, districts with their own character, workshops glowing around furnaces, warehouses filled with goods brought up from deeper tunnels, and markets where nearly anything could be found if one possessed the coin, the connections, or the willingness to ask the right merchant.

The Bazaar was a maze of voices and smells. In Duthcloim, merchants and craftsmen crowded together, trading food, weapons, fungi, cloth, poisons, curiosities, and stranger things whose origins were better left unasked. Slaves carried crates and baskets through the press of bodies, while a duergar merchant might haggle with a drow over a shipment and a goblin servant waited behind him with the next load. Guards watched the crowds without seeming to watch them at all.

In Narbondellyn, the streets widened, giving way to elegant estates, fashionable shops, skilled artisans, and houses wealthy enough to make their status visible in stone and metal. It was a district where reputation could be displayed as easily as jewelry, and where a passing glance at a doorway might tell an experienced drow more about its owner than the house name ever would.

Beyond it stood Qu'ellarz'orl, screened from much of the city by enormous mushrooms and guarded by distance as much as by force. The greatest noble houses occupied its heights, while commoners and outsiders did not wander there by accident.

Menzoberranzan had places for nearly everyone. It simply did not give everyone the same place.

The city was not silent. Footsteps crossed the bridges, merchants called from shadowed stalls, servants carried messages beneath the eyes of armed guards, and smiths struck heated metal. Somewhere deeper in the cavern, a slave shouted and was silenced. Noble processions moved through the traffic, and the crowd opened before them without needing to be told.

People knew how to make room. A merchant noticed a house emblem before he noticed the person wearing it; a servant recognized the rank of a passing noble by clothing, weapons, and the way those around her behaved; and a warrior saw a priestess approaching and changed his posture before she had reached him. Children learned these things without lessons because everyone around them already knew.

The city had been built in darkness by people who had learned to live inside it so completely that they no longer thought of darkness as the absence of anything. It concealed, protected, and gave the unwary somewhere to disappear; it was as natural to the drow as the stone beneath their feet—and just as unforgiving when someone misjudged its weight.

The darkness was also one of their oldest advantages. Drow eyes saw where surface eyes failed, heat marked the warm body hiding behind a cold wall, and a person standing still was still a person. Even a hand around a weapon gave off enough warmth to betray it.

The darkness was not empty. It was information.

Every shadow could contain a threat, an opportunity, or nothing at all, and the drow had spent thousands of years learning the difference.

They were not always what they had become. The stories taught to drow children told of an older people, elves who had once lived beneath open skies before Lolth and the conflict among the elven gods led her followers away from the surface. Those stories changed depending on who told them. Priestesses spoke of betrayal and divine purpose; others remembered only that the drow had been driven down and that their enemies had stolen the world above.

Whatever the truth of those ancient days, the drow inherited the hatred.

It came before any child could understand the history behind it. Surface elves were not strangers in the way humans were strangers; they were kin who had become enemies, and the wound was old enough that no living drow could remember when it had first opened.

The drow lived in centuries. A single drow lifetime could outlast the rise and fall of human kingdoms, could watch generations of surface folk be born and die while the same dark eyes looked on. Their long lives gave them time to preserve grudges, refine crafts, remember names, and wait for opportunities that shorter-lived peoples would consider absurdly distant.

They also gave the city an unusually long memory.

A ruined doorway might still bear the insignia of a house that had fallen centuries ago. An old passage might have been widened because some dead Matron Mother had once needed her procession to pass through it, while a name spoken in one district might still carry the weight of a murder committed generations earlier.

Menzoberranzan remembered. Sometimes the living did not.

In those long years, the drow also carried the old world's gifts into the dark and bent them to their own uses. As children grew, they learned the powers inherent to their kind: lights appearing at a gesture, darkness gathering where it was commanded, magic capable of revealing a hidden target or lifting a body from the ground.

These were not wonders to them. They were birthright.

The city did not teach a drow child to marvel at such things; it taught the child to use them. A drow who could summon light learned that light could distract, a drow who could summon darkness learned that darkness could hide, and a drow who could make another body glow learned that being seen at the wrong moment could be as dangerous as being invisible.

Magic was part of life long before it became a profession.

The dark was never empty to them. They saw further into it than any surface eye, and they read it like a language. It was where they lived, where they had buried themselves, and where they had learned how to kill.

Spiders filled the city's architecture: carved into pillars, worked into doorways, raised above temples, and woven into the emblems of many houses. Some were beautiful, some grotesque, and some were so large that a child could stand beneath their carved bodies and still feel small. They appeared on jewelry, weapons, walls, shrines, and banners, serving as warnings, symbols, and reminders.

A drow learned early that there were some things it was wiser to observe than to touch, even when they were only stone. The city offered many such lessons.

Its towers belonged to houses. Its bridges carried traffic under the watch of whoever controlled the territory around them. Its markets belonged to merchants who understood which customers could be cheated and which customers could not. Its highest districts belonged to families whose names could change the way a room breathed.

Every path through Menzoberranzan passed through someone's power.

The city did not explain that power. It demonstrated it, and everyone who lived there learned to watch.
