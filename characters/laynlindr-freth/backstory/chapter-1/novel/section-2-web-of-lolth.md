# 1.2 — The Web

Two male drow soldiers stood beneath an arcade of black stone while the traffic of Menzoberranzan passed around them. One wore the insignia of an armed spider, each of its eight legs gripping a different weapon; the other wore two spiders facing one another in a dark metal clasp at his shoulder. They had stopped to settle the delivery of a small alchemical consignment.

The soldier with the armed spider inspected the vessel before handing it back. “The third arrived with sediment. Four measures are due.”

The other soldier kept his gaze on the seal. “It left my stores intact. Three.”

The first soldier's thumb paused against the vessel's rim. “Then someone opened it between our stores.”

“Name the handler,” the second replied, his tone cool.

“We examined it before storage. Four.”

The soldier with the two-spider insignia gave a slight nod. “Very well. Have the next consignment arrive clean.”

They moved on, neither having raised his voice. There was no need. Each had tested the other's claim, neither willing to absorb the cost, and the matter had ended only when one decided the argument was no longer worth continuing. Courtesy had served its purpose without becoming trust.

The street was busy with the ordinary business of the city: merchants called to customers, slaves carried crates between stalls, and soldiers moved through the traffic in the colors of their Houses. Then, gradually, the noise began to change. A merchant lowered his voice, a servant stepped against a wall, and two soldiers cleared the center of the street.

The priestesses were coming.

They appeared beneath the violet lights at the far end of the avenue, moving slowly through the traffic. They did not call for the street to be cleared because they did not need to. Drow moved aside before they approached, leaving a broad path through the market as merchants drew back their goods, slaves stopped where they stood and lowered their heads, soldiers stepped aside, and noble drow gave ground without protest.

The priestesses wore spiders openly: carved into collars, worked into metal, and hung from chains at their waists. Several carried ritual vessels; others carried banners.

The first banner bore the insignia of Baenre.

The second carried the mark of Teken'duis.

The two soldiers lowered their eyes.

A high priestess of Baenre walked beneath the first standard, surrounded by attendants and lesser priestesses. The Matron Mother of Baenre had not come; she had no need to attend a lesser House's punishment herself. A priestess of sufficient rank was enough to carry Baenre's authority, and her presence made the arrangement plain: the First House had been asked to stand as the hand of Lolth, while Teken'duis came to answer before it.

The Matron Mother of Teken'duis followed beneath her own banner, walking close behind the Baenre priestess. The difference between them was not measured only by the distance between their feet.

Baenre was first.

Teken'duis followed.

The street understood.

The procession continued into older passages beneath the noble quarter, where the markets thinned behind them, the walls narrowed, the light weakened, and spiders appeared more frequently in the stone. The two soldiers followed far enough behind that none of the priestesses needed to acknowledge them.

The soldier with the armed spider kept his eyes on the procession. “The charge?”

The other soldier glanced once toward the Teken'duis banner. “Blasphemy.”

Neither man asked the question that would have mattered more. The answer was already moving toward the chamber.

The passage opened into a circular chamber cut from the rock, and the soldiers stopped near the entrance. From where they stood, the entire chamber was visible: a low stone altar upon a raised circular platform at its center, black candles burning around it, and behind the altar a great carving of Lolth, her beautiful female form descending into the body of a monstrous spider, her legs spreading across the wall. The banners of Baenre and Teken'duis stood on either side of the altar.

The Baenre high priestess took her position before it, while the Matron of Teken'duis knelt behind her and other priestesses formed a wide circle around the platform.

Only then was the subject brought into the chamber.

He was a male drow of Teken'duis, dressed in formal House clothing, his wrists bound before him. His eyes were open, but he did not look at the soldiers or at the crowd; his movements were precise and obedient, as if every step had already been decided for him, while a priestess rested two fingers against the side of his neck and guided him forward.

The magic holding him was subtle. His body obeyed, while his mind remained unreachable. That was preferable for everyone involved.

A condemned male who shouted accusations through the streets, begged for his life, or named those who had arranged his fall could humiliate his House before the entire city, and Teken'duis would not permit that.

The high priestess raised one hand, and the chamber fell silent. When she spoke, her voice carried beyond the altar and into the chamber where every witness could hear it.

“Spider Queen, judge the unworthy. Take what has failed us. Strengthen those who remain. Let every eye remember what becomes of those who turn from your web.”

The lesser priestesses repeated the final words together, their voices turning the prayer into a warning that belonged to everyone in the room. The Matron of Teken'duis lowered her head.

The charge was pronounced by a priestess at the altar.

“Blasphemy.”

The single word gave the punishment its lawful shape.

Lolth's worship was not separate from the law of the city. Her priestesses were not merely keepers of shrines; they were the authorities through whom the goddess's order was enforced, and a drow accused of blasphemy could be placed beneath their judgment regardless of rank.

But the accusation was only the lawful face of the matter.

Teken'duis had been careful for some time. Its offerings had diminished, its public displays of devotion had become less confident, and failures that might once have been attributed to bad judgment or an unlucky rival had begun to gather a more dangerous interpretation.

Perhaps Lolth was displeased.

For a lesser drow, that thought might mean fear of punishment. For a House, it meant survival.

A House could lose cargo and replace it, lose soldiers and recruit others, or suffer a humiliating defeat and spend years rebuilding its reputation. What it could not afford was for other Houses to believe that Lolth had withdrawn her favor.

In Menzoberranzan, success was never only success. A fortunate campaign could be called Lolth's blessing, a prosperous season could be offered as proof of devotion, and a rival's failure could be taken as evidence that the goddess preferred another House.

Every victory therefore had a second audience.

Lolth.

That was why Houses competed so fiercely for rank, influence, and reputation. Wealth mattered, soldiers mattered, and knowledge mattered, but all of them were means to the same greater end: remaining in the goddess's favor.

Teken'duis had decided to make that calculation visible.

The accused male might have been guilty. He might have been forced into whatever act had been called blasphemy. Another drow might have arranged the circumstances and placed the blame upon him.

It did not matter now.

The charge gave the House a lawful victim. The sacrifice gave the House an opportunity. The difference between the two was thin.

A Matron could punish a blasphemer. She could surrender the condemned to Lolth. She could claim that the goddess had demanded judgment. She could not simply kill a member of her House and call the act devotion.

Not openly.

The laws of the city gave the Houses rules for their struggle, while Lolth's religion gave those rules their purpose. A House could scheme, compete, and destroy its enemies, but it still needed to preserve the appearance that its actions belonged within the order established by the Spider Queen.

Teken'duis was walking that narrow line.

The subject was brought onto the platform. The high priestess stood before him, the Matron remained kneeling, and the Baenre and Teken'duis banners rose behind them, making the hierarchy plain for everyone to see.

Baenre had sent a priestess because its authority did not require the presence of its Matron. The First House could lend Lolth's judgment to a lesser House without yielding its own importance. Teken'duis had brought its Matron because the condemned male belonged to it, and because the House itself needed to be seen making the offering.

The two soldiers watched from the entrance.

The high priestess touched two fingers to the subject's forehead, and the spell released.

His breath caught, his eyes widened, and for the first time since entering the chamber, he understood where he was. He struggled against the hands holding him, but the priestesses did not move aside.

The Matron of Teken'duis watched him. Her face was rigid, not with pity and not at the thought of what would happen to him, but with an attention fixed on the high priestess and the altar beyond her. She was waiting to see whether Lolth would accept what Teken'duis had brought.

That was the true purpose of the ceremony.

The House did not offer the male because his death was necessary. It offered him because the House believed that losing him might be worth gaining the goddess's favor again.

If ten males had been certain to win back that favor, Teken'duis would have had little difficulty surrendering them. The difficulty was not compassion; it was proving that the sacrifices were punishments permitted by Lolth's law rather than killings committed simply because the House desired them.

That was the balance every ambitious House understood: enough cruelty to satisfy the goddess, and enough law to make the cruelty acceptable.

The priestesses began to sing, their voices rising around the platform in measured praise of Lolth's strength and judgment.

“Spider Queen, judge the unworthy,” the high priestess intoned.

The subject cried out.

“Take what has failed us,” the priestesses answered, their voices steady even as his broke against them.

The high priestess lifted a ceremonial blade.

The Matron remained motionless.

The blade entered the male's chest beneath the House insignia. He convulsed, and his cry broke through the chamber before the priestesses drowned it beneath their voices.

No one looked away.

The Baenre priestess held him until his struggling ceased. The Matron of Teken'duis watched until his body became still, her face showing no triumph, only waiting.

Then the high priestess withdrew the blade.

The first part of the ritual was finished.

Priestesses of House Teken'duis gathered around the altar and began a longer prayer. Now the House would wait beneath Lolth's gaze.

The high priestess of House Baenre had more important matters to attend to. She gave a final instruction to the priestesses and turned away from the altar, the Baenre banner following her from the chamber. She had done what the First House had been required to do.

The soldier with the two-spider insignia remembered that he also had to hurry. He left without offering a word of farewell because there was nothing to say that mattered enough to delay him.

He moved quickly through the old tunnels, passed back into the busier streets, and hurried toward the district where his House stood.

Menzoberranzan had already returned to its ordinary rhythm. Merchants resumed their arguments, slaves carried cargo, and soldiers changed patrols while noble drow went about their business beneath the same darkness that had hidden the ritual from most of the city.

The soldier continued toward the House district.

Ahead, the dark towers of a drow House rose from the stone. Its galleries climbed the rock in severe lines, narrow balconies overlooked the streets below, and weapons marked the approaches. The two spiders, the unmistakable insignia of House Freth, the 19th of Menzoberranzan, faced one another above the entrance, watching the ever-moving city.

The soldier passed beneath its emblem and disappeared into the galleries.

Behind him, the city continued as it always had.

Lolth did not need to stand in every street. Her priestesses carried her authority, her hierarchy determined who could command, who could punish, who must kneel, and who could be offered, and her Houses competed not merely against one another, but for the goddess's continued regard.

The web did not abolish chaos.

It gave chaos rules.

And the rules made the struggle serve Lolth.
