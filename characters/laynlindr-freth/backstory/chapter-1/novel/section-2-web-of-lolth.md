# Part I — The Child of House Freth

## I.1 — Beneath the Spider’s City

### I.1.2 — The Web

There was no separate place in Menzoberranzan for religion and life.

No one in the city stood outside the hierarchy chain.

Servants rushed aside when a male drow crossed their path. Among the males, a noble could make a soldier shiver with a single command. And a wise male noble—humiliated by a female more often than he would ever admit—kept his eyes away from a female's face.

The females ran the city.

The most powerful and feared of them were the priestesses, the chosen of their goddess. Matron Mothers, the wisest and often the cruelest of all, ruled each house and stood at the top—as far as any mortal could go.

Above everything mortal stood the dark goddess, the evil divinity who brought nothing but chaos, the bane of light itself.

A child learned all of this through posture before prayer. Before a child could recite the goddess's name, the child knew when to kneel, where to stand, and which direction to turn the eyes.

In the noble houses, children were brought to shrines cut into the stone. They watched adults lower themselves before carved images of their goddess. They watched priestesses raise their hands and offerings disappear into dark places. They listened as prayers were spoken in voices careful not to sound uncertain.

The children learned the gestures before they understood the words.

If one of them looked around during the prayer, a female hand closed on the back of the neck and drove the head down until the child's neck trembled against the grip. If the child was slow to learn, the whip made the lesson, cutting through the silence in front of everyone.

"Eyes down."

The voice did not need to be loud. The correction carried its own explanation.

The hand let go, and the child found the floor again. The others had gone still, their shoulders raised, their own gazes fixed on the stone. The priestess had already turned back to her prayer.

They learned that the goddess watched.

She rewarded strength and cunning. She exposed weakness. She favored those who understood the struggle for power and destroyed those who mistook mercy for safety. In Menzoberranzan, even a prayer carried the shape of a warning.

Her priestesses spoke with her authority. To disobey one was not merely to disobey another drow. It was to place oneself against the goddess who stood behind her. No child needed that distinction explained more than once.

She was above them.

Her priestesses were above them.

The noble houses stood beneath the priestesses, each with its own divisions of rank, privilege, blood, and usefulness. Every drow occupied a place in that order, and every drow was expected to understand it before someone else made the understanding painful.

The drow did not need to be reminded that the goddess was watching.

They reminded one another.

None of this was considered strange.

It was the order of things.

And in Menzoberranzan, the order of things was not questioned lightly.

The city taught its lessons through behavior rather than explanation.

A noble female could speak while a room waited.

A priestess could enter without saying a word and change the posture of everyone inside the room.

A servant could carry information farther than a warrior could carry a sword. A servant heard things, carried messages, and learned when silence was worth more than honesty.

A male could possess noble blood and still be expected to move aside when a woman approached. A male learned very early that there were rooms he should not enter, conversations he should not interrupt, and eyes he should never meet.

Power did not always announce itself.

Sometimes it was a command.

Sometimes it was a threat.

Sometimes it was simply a person being allowed to speak while everyone else remained silent.

Children learned to watch for the difference.

The goddess who watched over all of it, who sat above every house and every priestess and every claim in the dark—she had a name. One not spoken carelessly.

Lolth.

The Spider Queen.
