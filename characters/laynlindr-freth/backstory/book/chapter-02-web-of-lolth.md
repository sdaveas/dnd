## Chapter 2—The Web

Two male drow soldiers stood beneath an arcade of black stone at the edge of a market square while the traffic of Menzoberranzan passed around them. One wore the insignia of an armed spider, each of its eight legs gripping a different weapon; the other wore two spiders facing one another in a dark metal clasp at his shoulder. They had stopped to settle the delivery of a small alchemical consignment.

The soldier with the armed spider held up a small case of dark glass vials and let it catch the light. “Your House is busier than usual, I see.”

The soldier with the two spiders set a purse of gold in the other’s free hand before he took the case. “And yours wealthier.”

He slipped the case into an inner pocket of his cloak, and his eyes passed once along the arcade to see who might be watching. “Wealthy enough, I trust, to stop asking questions.”

The soldier with the armed spider weighed the purse without looking at it. The look he gave the other held nothing courteous at all. “As soon as you are more mindful of whom you address.”

Courtesy had served its purpose without becoming trust.

The square was busy with the ordinary business of the city: merchants called to customers, slaves carried crates between stalls, and soldiers moved through the traffic in the colors of their Houses. At its center, on a raised circular platform, stood a public altar of Lolth, and the market flowed around it without a glance. Then, gradually, the noise began to change. A merchant lowered his voice, a servant stepped against a wall, and a pair of guards cleared a path toward the altar.

The priestesses were coming.

They appeared beneath the violet lights at the far side of the square, moving slowly through the traffic. They did not call for the square to be cleared because they did not need to. Drow moved aside before they approached, leaving a broad path through the market as merchants drew back their goods, slaves stopped where they stood and lowered their heads, soldiers stepped aside, and noble drow gave ground without protest.

The priestesses wore spiders openly: carved into collars, worked into metal, and hung from chains at their waists. Several carried ritual vessels; others carried banners.

The first banner bore the insignia of Baenre. The second carried the mark of Teken’duis.

The two soldiers lowered their eyes.

A high priestess of Baenre walked beneath the first standard, surrounded by attendants and lesser priestesses. The Matron Mother of Baenre had not come; she had no need to attend a lesser House’s punishment herself. A priestess of sufficient rank was enough to carry Baenre’s authority, and her presence made the arrangement plain: the First House had been asked to stand as the hand of Lolth, while Teken’duis came to answer before it.

The Matron Mother of Teken’duis followed beneath her own banner, walking close behind the Baenre priestess.

Baenre was first.

Teken’duis followed.

The square understood.

The procession crossed to the altar at the center of the square. Behind it rose a great statue of Lolth, her beautiful female form descending into the body of a monstrous spider, her stone legs spreading over the platform. The priestesses set black candles burning around the altar and raised the banners of Baenre and Teken’duis on either side of it.

No one left the square while the priestesses held it. The two soldiers remained beneath the arcade, where they could see all of it.

“The charge?” the soldier with the armed spider asked, without looking at him.

The other soldier glanced once toward the Teken’duis banner. “Blasphemy.”

Neither man asked whether it was true.

The Baenre high priestess took her position before the altar, while the Matron of Teken’duis knelt behind her and other priestesses formed a wide circle around the platform.

Only then was the subject brought into the square.

He was a male drow of Teken’duis, dressed in formal House clothing, his wrists bound before him. His eyes were open, but he did not look at the soldiers or at the crowd; his movements were precise and obedient, as if every step had already been decided for him, while a priestess rested two fingers against the side of his neck and guided him forward.

The magic holding him was subtle. His body obeyed, while his mind remained unreachable. That was preferable for everyone involved.

A condemned male who shouted accusations through the streets, begged for his life, or named those who had arranged his fall could humiliate his House before the entire city, and Teken’duis would not permit that.

The high priestess raised one hand, and the square fell silent. When she spoke, her voice carried to every witness beneath its arcades.

“Spider Queen, judge the unworthy. Take what has failed us. Strengthen those who remain. Let every eye remember what becomes of those who turn from your web.”

The lesser priestesses repeated the final words together, their voices turning the prayer into a warning that belonged to everyone in the square. The Matron of Teken’duis lowered her head.

The charge was pronounced by a priestess at the altar.

“Blasphemy.”

The single word gave the punishment its lawful shape.

But that was only the face of the matter.

For some time, the city had watched Teken’duis with suspicion. Its offerings had diminished, its public displays of devotion had become less confident, and failures that might once have been attributed to bad judgment or an unlucky rival had begun to gather a more dangerous interpretation.

Perhaps Lolth was displeased.

A House could lose cargo and replace it, lose soldiers and recruit others, or suffer a humiliating defeat and spend years rebuilding its reputation. What it could not afford was for other Houses to believe that Lolth had withdrawn her favor. In Menzoberranzan, every victory had a second audience.

Lolth.

The accused male might have been guilty. He might have been forced into whatever act had been called blasphemy. Another drow might have arranged the circumstances and placed the blame upon him.

It did not matter now.

A Matron could punish a blasphemer. She could surrender the condemned to Lolth. She could not simply kill a member of her House and call the act devotion.

Not openly.

The subject was brought onto the platform. The high priestess stood before him, the Matron remained kneeling, and the Baenre and Teken’duis banners rose behind them, making the hierarchy plain for everyone to see. The two soldiers watched from beneath the arcade.

The high priestess touched two fingers to the subject’s forehead, and the spell released.

His breath caught, his eyes widened, and for the first time since entering the square, he understood where he was. He struggled against the hands holding him, but the priestesses did not move aside.

The Matron of Teken’duis watched him. Her face was rigid, not with pity and not at the thought of what would happen to him, but with an attention fixed on the high priestess and the altar beyond her. She was waiting to see whether Lolth would accept what Teken’duis had brought.

The House did not offer the male because his death was necessary. It offered him because the House believed that losing him was worth even a possibility of gaining the goddess’s favor again.

Every ambitious House understood the balance: enough cruelty to satisfy the goddess, and enough law to make the cruelty acceptable.

The priestesses began to sing, their voices rising around the platform in measured praise of Lolth’s strength and judgment.

“Spider Queen, judge the unworthy,” the high priestess intoned.

The subject cried out.

“Take what has failed us,” the priestesses answered, their voices steady even as his broke against them.

The high priestess lifted a ceremonial blade. The Matron remained motionless.

The blade entered the male’s chest beneath the House insignia. He convulsed, and his cry broke across the square before the priestesses drowned it beneath their voices.

No one looked away.

The Baenre priestess held him until his struggling ceased. The Matron of Teken’duis watched until his body became still, her face showing no triumph, only waiting.

Then the high priestess withdrew the blade. The first part of the ritual was finished.

Priestesses of House Teken’duis gathered around the altar and began a longer prayer. Now the House would wait beneath Lolth’s gaze.

The high priestess of House Baenre had more important matters to attend to. She gave a final instruction to the priestesses and turned away from the altar, the Baenre banner following her from the square. She had done what the First House had been required to do.

As the banner passed out of sight, the crowd loosened, and the soldier with the two-spider insignia moved at once. The case beneath his cloak had been expected below before the procession ever closed the square, and his House did not accept a procession as an excuse. He left without offering a word of farewell because there was nothing to say that mattered enough to delay him.

He cut through the crowd, left by the first open street, and hurried toward the district where his House stood.

Behind him, the square had already returned to its ordinary rhythm. Merchants resumed their arguments, slaves took up their cargo, and the traffic of the market flowed around the altar once more, past the priestesses of Teken’duis still praying over what their House had given.

Ahead, the dark towers of a drow House rose from the stone. Its galleries climbed the rock in severe lines, narrow balconies overlooked the streets below, and weapons marked the approaches. The two spiders, the unmistakable insignia of House Freth, nineteenth House of Menzoberranzan, faced one another above the entrance, watching the ever-moving city.

The soldier passed beneath its emblem and disappeared into the galleries.

Lolth did not need to stand in every street. Her priestesses carried her authority, her hierarchy determined who could command, who could punish, who must kneel, and who could be offered, and her Houses competed not merely against one another, but for the goddess’s continued regard.

The web did not abolish chaos.

It gave chaos rules.

And the rules made the struggle serve Lolth.
