# Part I — The Child of House Freth

## I.2 — The Web Within House Freth

### I.2.1 — A Normal Day

At ten years old, Layn had learned that a day in House Freth did not begin with sunlight.

There was no sunlight beneath Menzoberranzan.

It began when the house changed its lights.

The dim violet glow in the sleeping corridors faded toward blue, and the magical lamps set into the walls brightened one by one. Servants moved through the passages carrying water, clothing, and messages. Somewhere above, a bell sounded once—not to mark morning, but to announce that the household had begun expecting things from its people.

Layn woke before the second bell.

He had learned to do that because being woken by someone else usually meant that he was already late.

His room was a narrow chamber set below the better apartments of the noble females. It was not a servant’s room. The walls were faced with smooth black stone, the bedding was clean, and a small carved spider watched from above the doorway. But the room was plain, and its plainness made the distinction clear. He was noble by blood. He was young, male, and unimportant by every other measure that mattered in House Freth.

He dressed in the clothes laid across the foot of his bed and folded the blanket before leaving. No one had asked him to do either thing. He had simply learned that servants complained less when they found a room in order, and that complaints eventually traveled upward.

The corridor outside was empty except for a single female servant carrying a tray.

She stopped when she saw him.

“The lower hall, male,” she said. “Do not keep the household waiting.”

“Yes.”

She lowered her eyes and continued past him.

The servant had not used his name. Few people in the house did.

Layn walked toward the lower hall.

The first meal was not a family gathering in the way surface stories described such things. No one sat together because they wanted one another’s company. The household ate where rank and schedule placed them, and conversation moved toward whoever held the most authority in the room.

Layn took his usual place at the far end of the table, below the older males and behind the younger daughters. He kept his hands still while servants placed bitter tea and a dish of spiced cave fungus before him.

His elder cousin, Dreydre, sat three places closer to the head of the table. She had lived a century and already carried herself as though the house had begun making room for her future. Her posture was perfect. Her voice was quiet. She never had to repeat an order.

Greyanna sat beside her.

At sixty, Greyanna had not yet learned Dreydre’s control. She smiled too often when someone else was embarrassed, and her eyes lingered on small injuries with the curiosity of a child examining an insect.

Neither cousin looked at Layn when the Matron Mother entered.

The room changed around her.

Servants stepped away from the center aisle. The older males lowered their heads. The daughters straightened, each of them suddenly more attentive to the angle of her shoulders and the placement of her hands.

The Matron Mother crossed the hall surrounded by two priestesses and a pair of armed attendants. Her robes were the dark red of dried blood, embroidered with House Freth’s phoenix rising toward a dark-green moon. She did not hurry. The household adjusted itself to her pace.

Layn rose with everyone else.

He kept his gaze lowered, but he could see her feet pass through the edge of his vision. He waited for the familiar pause—the smallest sign that she had noticed him, even if she did not speak.

There was no pause.

The Matron Mother stopped beside Dreydre instead.

“The eastern gallery will receive the visitors,” she said. “You will be present.”

“Yes, Matron Mother.”

“And you will not let your cousin speak for you.”

Dreydre bowed her head. “Of course.”

The Matron Mother moved on.

Layn remained standing until the women had taken their places. He sat only when the older male beside him lowered himself into his chair.

The Matron Mother never looked in his direction.

That was normal.

He did not know whether she remembered that he existed. He did not know whether she had ever heard his name. When he was younger, he had sometimes tried to catch her attention by sitting straighter or answering a question before anyone else could. Each attempt had ended the same way. Her eyes passed over him as though he were part of the wall.

He had stopped trying.

The first lesson began after the meal.

House Freth educated its children inside the house whenever possible. The daughters were taught the relationships between noble families, the moods of priestesses, and the kinds of marriage that strengthened a bloodline. The males learned enough of those matters to avoid interfering with them.

Mistress Zilvrae taught Layn’s group in a room overlooking the training galleries.

She was a narrow-faced female with white hair braided tightly against her scalp. She had served House Freth for long enough that even the older daughters treated her corrections as something to be endured rather than challenged. Her lessons were called instruction, but her true subject was obedience.

That morning she placed twelve house emblems on a black table.

“Put them in order,” she said.

Layn studied the symbols. A silver blade. A broken chain. A spider above a crescent moon. A crown surrounded by nine points. Several he recognized from the bridges and gates of the city. The rest he knew from the household records he had been allowed to read.

He arranged them according to rank.

Zilvrae examined the table.

“Again.”

He looked at the symbols a second time. One of the smaller emblems had been turned slightly so that its identifying mark faced away from him. Layn corrected its position and moved it below the house it had once challenged.

“Again.”

There was no mistake left to find. He began to speak, explaining the old rivalry between two houses and the reason a lower-ranked family still received precedence at a particular ceremony. He had read the relevant passage the previous night.

“Enough,” Zilvrae said.

Layn stopped.

“You remember what you have been told. That is not the same as understanding it.”

“What did I misunderstand?”

The room went still.

Zilvrae’s eyes sharpened.

“You misunderstood the value of answering before you were asked.”

Layn lowered his head. “Yes, Mistress.”

Dreydre watched him from across the table. Greyanna had been turning a silver pin between her fingers. The pin stopped moving.

Zilvrae placed a shallow bowl on the floor.

“Cross the room,” she said. “Do not disturb the water.”

The other children went first. One stepped too quickly. Another glanced down and stumbled over his own robe. Dreydre crossed without making a sound, her feet finding the spaces between the loose stones.

Layn waited until his turn.

He saw the safest path immediately. The floor was not level. Three stones near the wall had been worn smooth by repeated traffic, and the fourth held a faint crescent of dust where its edge had shifted. He moved around them, placing each foot softly enough that the water remained still.

When he reached the other side, Zilvrae looked into the bowl.

“Acceptable.”

Layn waited.

That was all.

Stillness was the house’s discipline, not yet his shield. He did not know the water had any use beyond passing Zilvrae’s eye. Its worth would show that evening, though not in any lesson of Zilvrae’s.

Dreydre received a nod for crossing the same distance. Greyanna received a touch beneath the chin and a quiet word about grace. Layn received neither.

He returned to his place.

The lesson continued until the lamps changed again.

“You count quickly,” Zilvrae said.

“Then do not look as though you expect praise.”

He lowered his eyes.

The household had many ways of teaching a child what not to expect.

His cousins had more.

After the lesson, Greyanna found him in the passage outside the records room.

She planted herself in the middle of it and looked down at him.

“My bead is gone,” she said.

“I have not seen your bead.”

“You were alone in the records room. You came out with your sleeves closed.”

Layn opened them. They were empty.

Greyanna smiled and did not look at his hands.

“It was here this morning,” she said. “So were you.”

He had seen servants punished for theft. None of them had been guilty either.

So Layn went still.

It was the only thing he had. He stood as he had crossed the water that morning, breath slowed, hands open at his sides, nothing in him left moving that she could press against.

The panic sank beneath the stillness like sediment in water left alone.

And in the quiet it left, something pressed lightly behind his eyes.

It was not pain. He knew where the bead was. Not because he had seen it hidden, not because he had reasoned it out. The knowledge was simply there, finished and certain, like a thought that belonged to someone else.

He said it half to test whether it was true.

“In your right sleeve.”

Greyanna’s smile vanished.

Her right hand drifted to her sleeve, the way a hand drifts when it has nowhere else to go. She drew out the small black bead and held it where he could see it.

Dreydre stood behind her, half-hidden by the archway. For a moment, her expression held something Layn could not name. It was not anger. It was not surprise. It looked almost like fear.

Then it was gone.

“I put it there myself,” Greyanna said. “To see what you would say.”

Greyanna stepped close enough that Layn could smell the bitter oil in her hair.

“Do not become clever with me, boy.”

Dreydre lifted one finger.

Greyanna fell silent.

The older cousin took the bead from Greyanna’s fingers and turned it once in the lamplight.

“She misplaces things,” Dreydre said, not looking at Greyanna. “Perhaps you saw where this one fell.”

“I did not.”

“Then know where it is now.”

She held the bead up between two fingers, where the whole passage could see it.

“In a moment I will close my hands around it. You will say which one before they close. If you are wrong, she will have her thief.”

Layn looked at the bead.

Something pressed lightly behind his eyes.

It was not pain. It felt more like the moment before a thought formed, except the thought seemed to belong to someone else. The pressure moved from the bead to Dreydre, then toward him. He felt the direction of her attention before she spoke.

“Right.”

The bead had not moved. Neither hand had closed around it yet.

Dreydre closed both hands, then opened the right.

The bead was there.

Neither cousin spoke.

The pressure behind Layn’s eyes faded. He looked from the bead to Dreydre’s face.

“How did you—” Greyanna began.

Dreydre cut her off with a glance.

“He watched my hands,” she said.

Layn had not.

He did not say so.

Dreydre closed her fingers around the bead.

“You will not mention this.”

“Mention what?”

“Tonight.”

“It was only a bead.”

“Then there is no reason to mention it.”

She walked away. Greyanna followed, but not before giving Layn a look that made the passage feel narrower.

The rest of the day returned to its usual shape.

He learned which stairs were watched, which servants repeated everything they heard, and which doors could be opened without making a sound. He learned that the quickest route through the house was rarely the safest, and that being overlooked could be more useful than being noticed.

No one praised him for these discoveries.

No one needed to.

When the house lamps dimmed toward the end of the household’s cycle, Layn returned to his room.

The door was open.

He stopped before entering.

Two spiders rested on his bed.

They were not the small creatures carved into the walls or the delicate things that sometimes appeared in temple offerings. These were black and heavy-bodied, each with legs long enough to reach from the center of the blanket to its edge. Their movements were slow, deliberate, and disturbingly careful.

Dreydre stood beside the bed.

Greyanna sat on the windowsill, though there was no window beyond the dark opening—only a deep shaft crossed by a narrow bridge.

Layn’s throat tightened.

“Remove them,” he said.

Greyanna smiled.

“You know better than that.”

“They are in my bed.”

“They are sacred animals of Lolth.”

One of the spiders lifted its front legs and placed them on the folded blanket. Its body shifted with a faint, dry scrape.

Layn did not move.

Dreydre looked at him.

“If you kill them, the Spider Queen will know.”

“They are not temple spiders.”

“You would question what is sacred?”

“I only want them out.”

“Drive them away, then,” Greyanna said. “Put them in the corridor. Let them wander into the servants’ quarters. Let them crawl into someone else’s bed.”

Her smile widened.

“When the house suffers for your blasphemy, we will tell the Matron Mother who caused it.”

Layn looked toward the corridor.

No servant had come. No priestess had passed. His cousins had chosen a time when the room belonged to them.

“Does the Matron Mother know they are here?” he asked.

Dreydre’s expression hardened.

“The Matron Mother does not concern herself with a male child’s bedding.”

“Then why would she care if I moved them?”

“Because she would care about the consequences.”

The answer was delivered too quickly.

Layn looked at his cousins. Neither of them looked toward the upper galleries where the Matron Mother’s chambers lay.

He understood only that he was being watched.

“Lie down,” Dreydre said.

“I do not want to.”

Greyanna slid from the sill.

“You are a male in House Freth. Wanting is not a reason.”

Dreydre opened the door wider.

“Lie down, boy.”

Layn stepped into the room.

He removed his boots and climbed onto the bed without taking his eyes from the spiders. The first moved when the mattress dipped beneath his weight. Its legs touched his wrist.

He flinched.

“Still,” Dreydre said.

The word carried no anger. That made it worse.

Layn pressed his hands flat against the blanket. The spider climbed across his arm, its weight light enough to be almost gentle. The second followed, crossing his chest and pausing below his throat.

His heart beat so hard that he was certain his cousins could see it.

He tried to remember the lesson from the lower hall. A room. A table. Twelve symbols arranged according to rank. He tried to think of the city above the house, of bridges and carved stone, of anything larger than the bed beneath him.

The pressure behind his eyes returned.

For one instant the room seemed to draw inward. The spider on his chest stopped moving. Its legs folded against its body as though it had struck an invisible barrier.

Dreydre saw it.

Layn knew she saw it because her breathing changed.

Then the pressure vanished, and the spider continued toward his throat.

Neither cousin spoke.

They left him there when the household lamps dimmed again, closing the door behind them.

Layn remained still until the spiders had finished exploring the bed. He did not kill them. He did not drive them away. He did not call for a servant.

He lay beneath the sacred animals of Lolth and listened to the house move around him.

Above him, the Matron Mother continued her evening without knowing—or without choosing to know—where he was.

In the darkness, Layn tried to make himself smaller.

He was ten years old.

He had already begun learning how to disappear.
