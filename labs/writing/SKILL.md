# Writing Team Skill

## Scope

This guide applies to the novel markdown, table of contents, narrative notes, and story continuity work under `characters/laynlindr-freth/backstory/`.

## Story authority

- `backstory/notes.md` records decisions, canon constraints, and open questions.
- `backstory/toc.md` records the planned structure and drafting status.
- `chapter-*/novel/section-*.md` contains approved prose.
- `source/` contains reference material and earlier source text; it is not automatically canonical.

When prose and notes disagree, flag the mismatch before making a consequential change. A new scene must earn its place in the TOC and preserve established continuity.

## Canon grounding

Ground every section in the established Forgotten Realms setting. Use the [Forgotten Realms Wiki](https://forgottenrealms.fandom.com/) before adding names, houses, locations, chronology, relationships, or setting customs. Prefer canon where it exists; mark additions as **OUR STORY** and leave unresolved details as **UNKNOWN**.

The wiki is a research index, not an infallible authority. Follow its cited primary sources for consequential claims, watch for timeline or source conflicts, and record the page URL in `notes.md` or the relevant research note. Never overwrite a published event to make Layn's story easier; fit Layn into the gaps.

## Narrative principles

- Use Layn as the central character, but allow complete subsections to follow another character when that reveals motives Layn cannot see.
- Keep viewpoint changes at subsection boundaries; avoid abrupt head-hopping.
- Reveal drow society progressively through lived scenes: city, religion and culture, house hierarchy, family pressure, then institutions.
- Begin with the concrete and observable before explaining its wider meaning.
- Let readers infer the hierarchy through posture, permission, speech, punishment, and who is ignored.
- Characters may understand more than Layn, but Layn's own thoughts must fit his age and circumstances.
- Drow dialogue should reflect rank, ego, danger, and self-interest. Do not make characters explain the entire plan for the reader.
- Other characters should not casually call him “Layn” when the established scene calls for “male”, “boy”, or another status-based address. The narrator may call him Layn.
- Keep the prose original. Apply the underlying craft principles below, but never imitate R. A. Salvatore's wording, sentence patterns, or distinctive voice.

## Salvatore-informed direction

These are practical principles distilled from Salvatore's public interviews, adapted for this project rather than treated as a style-copying recipe.

- **Character growth is the engine.** Battles, betrayals, lessons, and discoveries are catalysts for a character to change. A scene should leave Layn, or the viewpoint character, with a changed understanding, decision, relationship, or capability.
- **Put the character under moral or emotional pressure.** Build scenes around what the character wants, what blocks it, what choice becomes unavoidable, and what the choice costs. The external plot should force an internal question.
- **Let motivation explain contradiction.** If a character behaves unexpectedly, investigate the fear, desire, pain, loyalty, or belief underneath it. Do not flatten surprising behavior into a mistake merely because it complicates the plan.
- **Keep energy in the prose.** Alternate reflection and movement. When tension rises, shorten paragraphs, sharpen verbs, reduce explanation, and make each beat change the situation. Give the reader room to breathe after the pressure releases.
- **Make action legible and physical.** Track position, balance, reach, timing, attention, and consequence. A fight is not a list of attacks; every movement should create or close an opening and reveal something about the combatant.
- **Give every important character a distinct way of acting.** Layn's stealth, daggers, tools, athletics, and psionic flashes should feel like parts of his history and temperament, not a pasted-on character sheet.
- **Use D&D mechanics as story constraints.** Spells, feats, proficiencies, and limitations should create risk, opportunity, or texture. Let a reader recognize the mechanics without interrupting the scene for a rules explanation.
- **Let discovery happen during drafting.** Keep the section's purpose, emotional destination, and continuity fixed, but allow the exact beat, gesture, line, or tactical turn to emerge from the characters' motives. Revise only when the discovery contradicts established facts.
- **Make ethics visible through choices.** Heroism or corruption is shown by what a character does when action hurts, costs status, or offers no immediate reward. Layn's rejection of drow society should be dramatized through decisions, not explained as a label.
- **Prefer clarity over ornament.** Use vivid, specific details and readable syntax. The prose should move cleanly when read aloud and remain accessible without becoming simplistic.

## Dialogue and narration

These rules capture the high-level effects we want from Salvatore's drow material without reproducing his phrasing or voice.

- **Dialogue is a contest of intention.** Before writing an exchange, define what each speaker wants immediately, what they refuse to say, and what they want the other person to believe. Every line should seek information, apply pressure, conceal weakness, change leverage, or force a choice.
- **Keep the power structure audible.** Rank changes word choice, directness, names, honorifics, interruptions, silence, and who is allowed the final word. A drow may insult someone without raising her voice; politeness can be a threat.
- **Let subtext do work.** Characters should rarely announce the whole plan. They circle it, test one another, reinterpret a detail, and arrive at a conclusion through competing motives. The reader should understand the plan because the exchange demonstrates it.
- **Use clear attribution and action beats.** Fast exchanges must remain easy to follow aloud. Use a name, a concise speech tag, or a physical action when the speaker could become ambiguous. Never sacrifice clarity to avoid a necessary attribution.
- **Make speech character-specific.** A priestess, frightened child, calculating cousin, weapon master, and exhausted soldier should not share the same sentence shape. Give each a preferred level of detail, rhythm, vocabulary, and way of avoiding the truth.
- **Allow silence to be a line.** A refusal to answer, a delayed glance, a hand tightening on a weapon, or a room returning to work can carry more authority than an explanation.
- **Narrate with a mobile camera, not a lecture.** Start in physical space, move close to the viewpoint character's attention, then widen only when the story needs social or historical scale. Omniscience should deepen the scene rather than pause it for an encyclopedia entry.
- **Make moral meaning emerge from behavior.** Do not label a character cruel, brave, or corrupted when a gesture, choice, or consequence can prove it. Let the reader feel the gap between what drow society calls virtue and what Layn experiences as monstrous.
- **Use contrast as a signature tool.** Pair ritual with gore, tenderness with danger, beauty with decay, or calm speech with irreversible violence. The contrast should expose the culture and the character at the same time.


## Dialogue execution standard

The following rules refine the dialogue principles above and apply during drafting and revision.

- **Physically inhabit dialogue.** Whenever practical, dialogue should occur through a character's actions, posture, expression, movement, tone, or reaction rather than existing as a sequence of detached quotations. The reader should experience the conversation as something happening between physical people in a physical space.
- **Communicate three things at once.** Important dialogue should make clear what is being said, who is saying it, and how that person is behaving while saying it. Use action beats, attribution, or surrounding context to achieve this without making the prose mechanical.
- **Use action beats for meaning, not decoration.** A gesture or movement should reveal character, hierarchy, tension, restraint, emotion, subtext, or a change in the physical situation. Do not add a mannerism merely because a quotation needs something attached to it.
- **Do not ban simple dialogue tags.** “Said,” “asked,” and “replied” are useful when they are the clearest and least distracting option. The goal is not to eliminate dialogue tags, but to avoid repetitive chains of quotation + attribution when behavior can carry the scene more naturally.
- **Avoid stage-play dialogue.** Several consecutive quotations with no physical, emotional, or environmental response should be treated as a signal to inspect the passage. Add behavior only where it contributes meaning; do not mechanically attach an action to every line.
- **Let manner reflect character.** The same situation should produce different physical and verbal behavior from different characters. A restrained character may reveal pressure through stillness, a change of grip, a pause, or a precise movement rather than an explicit emotional declaration.
- **Let behavior carry subtext.** When a character's action contradicts, qualifies, or deepens their words, prefer that tension over explaining the contradiction to the reader.
- **Preserve readability aloud.** In fast exchanges, action beats and attribution should clarify speaker changes without interrupting momentum. If a conversation becomes difficult to follow when read aloud, fix attribution and physical orientation before adding more description.
- **Do not over-choreograph.** Characters do not need to move, gesture, or emote continuously. Stillness, silence, and the absence of a reaction are deliberate forms of behavior and can be more powerful than constant motion.

## Vivid, graphic, eloquent execution

Use these as craft targets for the prose. They describe the desired effect, not a request to reproduce Salvatore's exact voice.

- **Make the world tactile.** Ground each important beat in two or three precise sensory details: the cold of worked stone, the drag of fabric, the metallic taste of fear, the smell after magic, the pressure of darkness, or the sound that makes a room go still.
- **Choose images with purpose.** Prefer one strong, concrete image that reveals mood, status, or danger over a pile of decorative comparisons. Let architecture, clothing, weapons, light, and bodies carry meaning.
- **Be graphic when consequence demands it.** Show the physical truth of violence, punishment, injury, and fear when softening it would weaken the scene. Use selected details—texture, sound, smell, movement, aftermath—rather than an indiscriminate gore catalogue.
- **Make violence causal and readable.** Establish intent, show the action, show its immediate physical effect, then show how witnesses react or how the situation changes. The reader should always understand what happened and why it matters.
- **Write with kinetic verbs.** Prefer exact physical actions over abstract summaries: pivot, flinch, brace, fold, sever, recoil, slide, catch, hesitate. Every movement should reveal skill, fear, rank, or changing advantage.
- **Let eloquence come from contrast.** Place beauty beside cruelty, ritual beside horror, silence beside impact, or a calm sentence beside an appalling event. Drow elegance should never make the brutality less real; it should make the brutality more unsettling.
- **Control cadence.** Use longer, flowing sentences for scale, atmosphere, thought, and social observation. Break into shorter sentences as danger closes in. Use paragraph length as part of the tension.
- **Keep the viewpoint emotionally present.** Graphic detail is filtered through what the viewpoint character notices, understands, misunderstands, or refuses to acknowledge. The narration should not become a detached anatomy report.
- **End scenes on an image or changed truth.** A final sensory image, gesture, silence, or realization should make the section linger and show what cannot return to normal.
- **Do not confuse eloquence with purple prose.** Avoid constant similes, theatrical declarations, ornamental synonyms, and graphic details that do not alter character or consequence. Vividness must serve story pressure.

## Dark tone and splatter

The Underdark and House Freth may be genuinely dark. Graphic violence and splatter are welcome when they reveal the society, transform a relationship, or leave a lasting psychological and physical consequence.

- Show the impact, not only the weapon: rupture, spray, heat, smell, wet stone, torn cloth, shock, paralysis, and the silence that follows are all available tools.
- Let violence remain specific to the magic, creature, weapon, and social ritual involved. A priestess's punishment should not feel like a generic battlefield wound.
- Give the aftermath room. Blood on a sleeve, a servant cleaning the floor, a witness changing posture, or a child learning the wrong lesson can carry more horror than the strike itself.
- Use calm, ordinary reactions to intensify horror when that fits drow culture. Routine continuing around a brutal event is part of the violence.
- Vary the register: dread, disgust, shock, numbness, panic, fascination, and grim practicality should not all sound alike.
- Do not turn suffering into decoration. Graphic detail must change the viewpoint character, the room, the power balance, or the reader's understanding.
- Do not soften cruelty merely because it is unpleasant, but do not linger after the scene's dramatic truth has landed. The goal is impact, not a gore inventory.
- Keep the dark tone in contrast with beauty, humor, competence, ritual, and moments of fragile connection. Constant misery becomes flat; contrast makes the darkness visible.

### Section test

Before approving a section, answer five questions:

1. What does the viewpoint character want in this section?
2. What pressure makes the want difficult or dangerous?
3. What choice or realization changes the situation?
4. What is the immediate cost or consequence?
5. What is different by the final paragraph?

If the answer to the last question is “nothing,” the section probably needs a stronger turn.

## Continuity anchors

- Spiders in the city appear as carvings, architecture, symbols, and religious imagery; the streets do not swarm with walking spiders.
- Layn is a young noble male. Older noble males stand above him; women of the house and priestesses occupy higher authority.
- House Freth's cruelty should be shown through ordinary behavior and hierarchy, not melodramatic speeches.
- The sisters' suppression of Layn's possible psionic talent is driven by fear of what his power could mean for their status and by the danger of concealing it from the Matron Mother—not compassion for Layn.
- Drow birthright magic and later rogue abilities should appear naturally in action. A D&D reader may recognize the mechanics, while a casual reader experiences character development rather than a rules list.

## Drafting workflow

1. Read `notes.md`, `toc.md`, and the current neighboring sections.
2. State the scene purpose and the continuity facts it must establish.
3. Draft with concrete action, sensory detail, and readable paragraph rhythm.
4. Check names, ages, hierarchy, timeline, canon, and viewpoint.
5. Read the passage aloud or inspect it for breathless sentences and confusing dialogue.
6. Update the TOC or notes only when the structure or story decision actually changed.

Do not create audio or comic panels from an unstable passage. Mark prose approved first, then hand it to the adaptation teams.

## Batch mode

For a batch request, read the selected manifest under `labs/batches/`, review sections in its order, and aggregate only approved prose. Do not combine section files into a replacement manuscript unless the user explicitly requests a compiled reading copy; the individual section files remain canonical.
