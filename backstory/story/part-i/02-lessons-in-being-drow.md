# Part I — The Child of House Freth

## I.2 — Lessons in Being Drow

### I.2.1 — The Ways of Lolth

A child born beneath the earth learned many things before he understood that he was learning them.

He learned to walk without making noise. He learned which passages were safe and which belonged to someone more important. He learned that a raised voice from a priestess could empty a room faster than any command from a guard. He learned that the names of noble houses mattered, that clothing mattered, that posture mattered, and that a careless glance could be remembered long after the words that caused it had been forgotten.

Most of all, he learned the name of Lolth.

There was no separate place in Menzoberranzan for religion and life. Lolth was not a subject taught in a particular hour before the children returned to ordinary lessons. Her presence was woven through the city itself, through the spider carvings in stone, the symbols worn by priestesses, the rituals of the noble houses, and the rules by which rank was maintained.

The children of House Freth were taught this early.

They were taught that Lolth watched.

They were taught that she rewarded strength and cunning, that weakness invited destruction, and that obedience to her priestesses was not merely obedience to another drow. The priestesses spoke with the authority of the goddess, and to challenge that authority was to invite consequences that no child needed explained twice.

The lesson was simple because it needed to be simple.

Lolth was above them.

Her priestesses were above them.

The noble houses stood beneath the authority of the priestesses, and within each house there were further divisions of rank, privilege, and usefulness. Every drow occupied a place in that order. Every drow was expected to understand it.

There were rituals, too. The children watched adults kneel, watched offerings disappear into sacred places, watched priestesses invoke the Spider Queen before matters of importance. They learned the gestures before they understood the words. They repeated prayers because everyone around them repeated prayers.

Layn did not question any of it.

He was too young to question a world that had never presented itself as anything other than the world.

When he looked at a priestess, he saw someone to be obeyed. When he heard Lolth's name, he understood that it belonged to something vast and dangerous. When punishment came, he understood that punishment was part of life. There was no contradiction for him to resolve.

And there was certainly no reason for Lolth to notice him.

The Spider Queen had no particular interest in the quiet boy of House Freth. There were countless drow beneath her web, and Layn was not remarkable enough to rise above them. Whatever potential lay dormant inside him remained buried beneath the expectations imposed upon him, and his sisters had already learned how to keep it there.

To Lolth, he was barely there at all.

For the moment, that suited everyone.

The priestesses had their chosen daughters. The warriors had their duties. The noble houses had their ambitions. The boys had their place in the order, and Layn was simply another boy expected to grow into it.

So he learned.

He learned to lower his eyes.

He learned that silence could be safer than honesty.

He learned that a drow did not survive by asking whether a rule was fair. A rule existed because someone with enough power had decided that it should exist, and the sensible response was to understand who benefited from it.

That lesson would prove more important than any prayer.

### I.2.2 — Houses and Power

Menzoberranzan was not a city held together by walls.

Its true structure was the network of houses that occupied its darkness.

The great noble houses were families, but the word *family* meant something different there. A house was blood, certainly, but it was also a political body, a military force, a collection of resources, a reputation, and a promise of protection. A house could rise because its members were strong, clever, useful, or simply fortunate. It could fall for the opposite reasons.

And when a house fell, blood alone could not save it.

The children of House Freth grew up surrounded by this understanding.

Their home was not merely where they slept. It was the physical expression of their house's position in the city. Guards stood where they were needed. Servants moved according to rank. Weapons and valuables were kept where they could be protected. Visitors were received according to their importance, and every arrival could carry information worth more than whatever goods they brought with them.

The children noticed these things because children noticed everything.

They noticed who was permitted to sit and who remained standing. They noticed which visitors caused conversation to become quieter. They noticed when a priestess entered a room and when a warrior stepped aside to let someone pass.

They learned that power did not always announce itself.

Sometimes it was a command.

Sometimes it was a threat.

Sometimes it was simply a person being allowed to speak while everyone else waited.

The drow understood hierarchy instinctively because they lived inside it. A noble female did not need to explain her authority to a male servant. A priestess did not need to justify a command to a warrior. A member of a powerful house could make another drow reconsider an insult with nothing more than a name.

House Freth itself occupied its place within that dangerous arrangement.

It was powerful enough to matter and vulnerable enough to remember that power was never permanent.

That was the lesson every noble house eventually learned.

Alliances were useful until they were not. Enemies could become allies when circumstances changed, and allies could become enemies the moment advantage demanded it. Information was currency. Favors were investments. Marriage could strengthen a position. A successful raid could enrich a house. A failed ambition could expose weakness.

Even among relatives, trust was never assumed.

A child could understand this without understanding politics.

Layn understood that people wanted things from one another. He understood that some drow could demand what others could only ask for. He understood that smiles were not necessarily friendly and that politeness did not mean kindness.

He did not yet have words for intrigue.

He had no need of them.

He saw its effects.

There were days when a servant who had been treated casually the day before suddenly became invisible. There were visitors whose presence changed the behavior of everyone around them. There were conversations that stopped when children entered a room, only to begin again after they had passed.

The city taught its lessons everywhere.

Menzoberranzan itself was an enormous demonstration of what happened when power became the foundation of society. Towers rose from the cavern floor, bridges crossed impossible distances, and homes were carved into the stone as though the rock itself had been forced to make room for those who commanded it. Magical light illuminated roads, halls, and grand estates, but darkness remained between them, swallowing whatever happened beyond the reach of attention.

Nothing about the city suggested safety.

Yet to the children who grew up there, it was home.

They played in its shadows. They memorized its paths. They learned where they could run and where they could not. They learned which adults were dangerous and which were merely unpleasant. They learned which names carried weight.

Layn learned the same things.

He simply learned to watch more carefully than he spoke.

He did not understand yet that this habit would follow him for the rest of his life.

### I.2.3 — The Academy

There came a point when the children of Menzoberranzan began to leave the protected spaces of their homes and enter the institutions that would shape the adults they were expected to become.

For the boys of the noble houses, the path was narrower than it was for the girls.

The daughters of noble families might be prepared for the service of Lolth and the authority that came with it. The sons were expected to become useful in other ways. They could serve as warriors, scouts, craftsmen, or pursue other disciplines according to their talents and the needs of their house.

Magic was powerful, but it was not merely another skill.

A male drow who possessed great magical ability could become valuable. He could also become dangerous.

The children were not given this distinction as a philosophical lesson. They encountered it through the behavior of the adults around them. Certain talents drew attention. Certain ambitions were encouraged. Others were watched more carefully.

Layn's sisters were among those watching him.

They had begun to notice things before he had learned to name them.

There were moments when Layn did something that should have been ordinary and yet left them looking at him a little longer than usual. A reaction that came too quickly. An awareness of something he should not have noticed. A strange pressure in his mind that seemed to answer instinct before thought.

It was not enough to make him exceptional.

It was enough to make his sisters cautious.

They understood something that Layn did not: whatever was developing inside him was not the kind of talent they wanted to see nurtured without supervision.

They were not going to allow the boy to discover what he might become on his own terms.

For now, however, the matter remained unspoken.

Layn simply began the process of becoming what a male drow of House Freth was supposed to become.

He was taught the use of weapons. He learned how to hold a blade properly, how to move through darkness, how to watch an opponent's hands rather than merely his face. He learned that strength was not the same thing as power and that a warrior who ignored the battlefield around him was already halfway to death.

The lessons were harsh because Menzoberranzan had little use for softness.

A mistake could hurt.

A second mistake could be humiliating.

A repeated mistake could make a teacher decide that a student was not worth the time required to correct him.

Layn adapted.

He was not the strongest boy in the room, nor the fastest, nor the most impressive. He did not need to be. He watched. He listened. When another student rushed forward, Layn noticed where the opening appeared. When a teacher demonstrated a movement, he remembered the movement itself as well as the reason behind it.

He learned that there was usually more happening than what people said aloud.

That lesson came naturally to him.

The same boy who had learned to keep his eyes lowered had learned to observe everything else.

The Academy became another part of his world, and for a time he accepted it without resistance. It was simply another set of rules to understand, another hierarchy to navigate, another place where adults decided what a boy should become.

His sisters were pleased with the direction his life was taking.

At least, they were for the moment.

The path toward the warriors' discipline kept him where they wanted him: useful, controlled, and far from the kind of education that might allow the strange potential they had sensed to grow unchecked.

They did not tell him why.

They did not need to.

In Menzoberranzan, children were rarely asked what they wanted to become.

They were told what they would become, and then they were taught how to survive it.

Layn accepted the lesson.

He trained.

He watched.

And somewhere beneath all the things the city was teaching him, something else was taking shape—a quiet habit of his own, one that no instructor had assigned and no priestess had demanded.

He was beginning to think for himself.

He did not yet know how dangerous that could be.
