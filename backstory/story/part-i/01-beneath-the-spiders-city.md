## I.1.1 — Menzoberranzan

There was no sky above Menzoberranzan.

There had never been one.

The city occupied a vast cavern deep beneath the earth, where stone rose higher than any tower could reach and darkness stretched beyond the limits of ordinary sight. The cavern roof disappeared into blackness, broken only by the faint glimmer of magical lights and the occasional glow of phosphorescent fungi clinging to the rock.

To the people who lived there, this was not darkness.

It was home.

The city rose from the cavern floor in impossible shapes. Great stalactites descended from above like stone spears, while enormous columns of rock climbed toward them from below. Bridges crossed the open spaces between them. Towers had been carved directly into the stone, their surfaces covered with delicate patterns, webs, and symbols.

Somewhere among those towers stood the great houses of Menzoberranzan.

They were not merely homes.

A house was a family, a fortress, a political faction, a source of protection, and a weapon.

Its walls sheltered its members from the dangers of the city.

Its name could open doors.

Its enemies could kill its children.

Its fortunes could rise for generations and vanish in a single night.

And above all of them stood Lolth.

The Spider Queen watched over her children.

At least, that was what every child of Menzoberranzan was taught.

Her presence could be found everywhere.

In the spider motifs carved into walls.

In the eight-legged creatures kept as sacred companions.

In the prayers spoken before meals.

In the lessons taught to children.

In the fear that accompanied every failure.

The drow did not need to be reminded that Lolth was watching.

They reminded one another.

And Menzoberranzan understood something that the surface world often forgot:

**power was not merely possessed. It had to be demonstrated.**

A noble house that appeared weak invited attack.

A priestess who showed hesitation invited contempt.

A servant who forgot their place might never make the same mistake twice.

A male who spoke when he should have remained silent could find himself wishing he had not.

None of this was considered unusual.

It was simply the order of things.

And the order of things was not questioned lightly.

House Freth occupied one of the great structures of the city.

It was not the highest-ranking house.

It did not need to be.

It was powerful enough to matter.

Powerful enough to have enemies.

Powerful enough that its name carried weight when spoken in the right rooms.

And inside that house lived a boy whom almost nobody noticed.

His name was Laynlindr Freth.

Layn.

He was young enough that the great games of the city were still beyond his understanding, but old enough to know that he occupied a strange place within his own family.

He knew which people deserved respect.

He knew which people deserved fear.

He knew which corridors he should not enter.

He knew when to lower his eyes.

He knew when to move aside.

And, most importantly, he knew when nobody was looking for him.

That last lesson came easily.

Nobody looked for Layn very often.

## I.1.2 — The Boy

Layn woke before the household did.

At least, before most of it.

There was always movement somewhere in House Freth. Servants passed through the corridors at all hours, carrying messages, food, weapons, clothing, or things Layn had no name for.

The house never truly slept.

Layn opened his eyes.

For a few moments he remained still.

The ceiling above him was black stone.

A faint violet glow entered his room from somewhere beyond the doorway, just enough to outline the furniture.

His bed.

His chest.

The small table beside him.

The doorway.

Nothing moved.

Layn sat up.

He listened.

Footsteps.

Far away.

A servant.

He climbed out of bed and dressed himself.

There were rules for everything in House Freth.

He did not need anyone to tell him this particular one.

**Do it yourself if you can.**

Needing another person was dangerous.

Depending upon another person was worse.

Layn fastened the final strap and stepped into the corridor.

A pair of servants passed him without speaking.

Neither bowed.

Neither needed to.

Layn was a male child of the house.

That gave him a place.

It did not give him importance.

He continued down the corridor.

As he walked, the architecture of House Freth revealed itself in the dim light.

Dark stone.

Smooth arches.

Carved spiders.

Weapons displayed along the walls.

The insignia of the house repeated everywhere.

The spider was never far away.

Layn had stopped noticing it.

That was what happened when you grew up in Menzoberranzan.

The things that would have seemed strange to an outsider became invisible.

The darkness.

The spiders.

The weapons.

The whispered conversations.

The way everyone watched everyone else.

The way a priestess could enter a room and change its atmosphere without saying a word.

Layn had never known another world.

He did not yet know there were children who woke in houses where their first thought was not:

*Who is going to be angry today?*

He found his sisters in one of the adjoining chambers.

They were already awake.

They were speaking quietly.

They stopped when Layn entered.

One of them looked at him.

The other didn't.

Layn waited.

Neither spoke.

He lowered his eyes and moved toward the table.

A servant brought food.

Layn ate.

His sisters talked again.

Not to him.

About him, perhaps.

He could never be certain.

That was another lesson.

Never assume that silence meant safety.

His mother was somewhere in the house.

Layn knew this because everyone knew where the Matron Mother was.

But he rarely saw her.

And when he did, she did not look at him.

Not once.

She ignored him altogether.

There were no arguments between them.

No affectionate moments.

No cruel words.

Nothing.

She simply never interacted with him.

She never even met his eyes.

For a child, that kind of absence was difficult to understand.

Layn eventually stopped trying.

## I.1.3 — A Normal Day

The day began as every day did.

Lessons.

Training.

Orders.

Meals.

Prayer.

Observation.

Menzoberranzan was a city where children learned early that survival depended on understanding people.

Not understanding them emotionally.

Understanding their position.

A priestess was not simply a woman.

A priestess was an agent of Lolth.

A noble was not simply a person.

A noble carried the weight of a house.

A servant was not simply someone who worked.

A servant knew things.

A male was not simply a person who happened to be male.

His place in the hierarchy was already determined.

These distinctions were not written on every wall.

They did not need to be.

They were taught through a thousand tiny interactions.

A male stepped aside.

A priestess walked through.

A servant waited.

A daughter spoke.

A son listened.

A Matron Mother decided.

And the household moved.

Layn watched.

He was good at watching.

It was one of the few things nobody had ever tried to take from him.

Later that day, Layn stood in one of the training chambers.

A wooden practice blade rested in his hand.

Across from him stood an older student.

The boy was larger.

Stronger.

More experienced.

The instructor gave the signal.

Layn moved.

The first strike came toward his shoulder.

He stepped aside.

The second came faster.

He caught it.

The third struck his ribs.

Layn stumbled.

The older boy smiled.

The instructor said nothing.

Layn raised his blade again.

He attacked.

Not because he believed he would win.

Because the lesson was not about winning.

It was about learning what happened when someone stronger than you wanted you to lose.

Layn watched his opponent's shoulders.

Then his hands.

Then his feet.

He stopped watching the blade.

The next strike came.

Layn moved before it arrived.

The wooden sword passed through empty space.

For the first time, the instructor's expression changed.

Only slightly.

Layn noticed.

He noticed everything.

He did not yet understand why.

Above the training chamber, beyond the walls of House Freth, Menzoberranzan continued its endless existence.

A merchant argued over a shipment.

A priestess delivered a command.

Two noble houses exchanged carefully chosen words.

A servant carried a message that could change someone's future.

Somewhere, a child was being taught to pray to Lolth.

Somewhere else, another child was being taught how to kill.

And far below the city, in the darkness beyond the reach of its lights, creatures moved through the tunnels.

Menzoberranzan did not pause for any of them.

It simply continued.

It had existed long before Laynlindr Freth was born.

It would exist after he was gone.

For now, Layn was only a boy in House Freth.

A male child among thousands.

A small piece in a city built from larger pieces.

And, as far as the Spider Queen was concerned—

**nothing worth noticing.**
