# Part I — The Child of House Freth

## I.1 — Beneath the Spider’s City

### I.1.1 — Menzoberranzan

There was no sky above Menzoberranzan.

There had never been one.

There was only stone, rising so far above the city that even the sharpest eyes could not find where the cavern ended and darkness began. Menzoberranzan occupied a vast, arrowhead-shaped cavern, its roof nearly a thousand feet above the floor. Great columns and jutting shelves of calcite divided the space into layers. Bridges and walkways crossed the emptiness between them, joining towers carved directly from the living rock.

Some houses clung to the cavern walls. Others rose from the floor in defensive spires. Paths crossed the roof overhead, where powerful magic held homes and galleries against the stone. From below, the highest districts seemed to hang above the city like a second, inverted world.

At the cavern's center stood Narbondel, a huge natural pillar of stone that served as the city's clock. Each morning, the Archmage warmed the stone to life. A band of heat climbed slowly up the pillar as the day passed, read from almost every street, bridge, and balcony by eyes that saw warmth as the surface folk saw color. When the heat reached its end and faded into cold, Menzoberranzan entered its long night.

Blue, green, and violet faerie fire burned across the city, outlining towers, gates, and house compounds in cold radiance. Pale fungi clung to the deeper walls and added their own weak glow. None of it defeated the darkness. The light only gave the darkness edges, leaving balconies, doorways, and entire stretches of roof to whatever waited beyond attention.

To someone from the surface, Menzoberranzan might have seemed to exist in perpetual night.

To the drow, it was simply home.

The city was not silent. Footsteps crossed the bridges. Merchants called from shadowed stalls. Servants carried messages beneath the eyes of armed guards. Noble processions moved through the traffic, and the crowd opened before them without needing to be told.

It had been built in darkness by people who had learned to live inside it so completely that they no longer thought of darkness as the absence of anything. It concealed. It protected. It gave the unwary somewhere to disappear. It was as natural to the drow as the stone beneath their feet—and just as unforgiving when someone misjudged its weight.

They were not always what they had become. The drow had been elves of the surface once, children of the sun and the open sky, before a corruption took them and drove them out of the light. They had followed a dark power down long tunnels into the deep earth, and generation by generation the deep earth had remade them, until the sun they had been born under became an enemy and the stone beneath them a home.

They did not remember the change. They remembered what they were taught: that they had been wronged by the surface elves, betrayed, driven down for a crime that had never been theirs. The hatred came before language. They learned to curse the name of their surface kin from the same mouths that taught them to speak, and the word their tongue made for those elves meant traitor. Of all their enemies, the drow counted the surface elves the deadliest—the single hatred they would keep alive across whole centuries of stone.

The drow lived in centuries. A single drow lifetime could outlast the rise and fall of human kingdoms, could watch generations of surface folk be born and die while the same dark eyes looked on. In those long years they carried the old world's gifts into the dark and bent them to their own uses. A drow child who could barely stand already called light to her fingers—dancing motes to tease the unwary, a shroud of darkness to swallow a room, the cold fire that painted a target so none could slip away. It was a birthright older than the Underdark, and below the light they had turned it into a set of weapons.

The dark was never empty to them. They saw further into it than any surface eye, and they read it like a language. It was where they lived, where they had buried themselves, and where they had learned how to kill.

Spiders filled the city's architecture: carved into pillars, worked into doorways, raised above temples, and woven into the emblems of many houses.

They were warnings, symbols, and reminders. A drow learned early that there were some things it was wiser to observe than to touch, even when they were only stone.

The city offered many such lessons.

Its towers belonged to houses. Its bridges carried traffic under the watch of whoever controlled the territory around them. Its darkness belonged to anyone clever enough to use it.

Everything else had to be claimed.
